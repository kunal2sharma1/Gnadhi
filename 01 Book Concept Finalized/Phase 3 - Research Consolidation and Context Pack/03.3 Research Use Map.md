## 1. Executive Summary

### Research-status finding

This report therefore maps:

- what can be checked against independent external historical scholarship and primary-record reporting;
- and what remains unestablished until the Claude packets are actually committed.

The checkpoint objective explicitly says the original concept must remain provisional and must survive serious criticism rather than being assumed correct. The strongest adversarial conclusion is that the original concept is presently **too compressed and causally expansive** to be accepted as written.

The sentence **“An ideology tried to kill Gandhi, but failed to kill what he represented, and is still trying to destroy his legacy”** bundles at least three different historical arguments:

1. a claim about the causes and ideological meaning of the assassination;
2. a claim about the survival of Gandhi after death;
3. a claim of continuing ideological and intentional continuity into later decades and the present.

The first claim is substantially supportable only if the book distinguishes Godse's personal ideological position from the larger ideological and organisational field around him. The second claim is supportable only after separating Gandhi's **image** from his **political philosophy, moral authority, political practice, and institutional influence**. The third claim is the weakest: evidence of later criticism, appropriation, symbolic conflict, or even Godse glorification does not by itself establish one continuous ideological project from 1948 to the present.

Independent scholarship supports both sides of the problem. Scholarship documents Godse's Hindu-nationalist ideological environment, his relationships with the Hindu Mahasabha and RSS, and the political significance of Partition-era grievances. At the same time, scholarship also documents post-1948 appropriation of Gandhi's image by actors who were not simply preserving the whole Gandhian programme, including Hindu nationalists who have selectively incorporated Gandhi while also criticising him and celebrating Godse. That makes **contested inheritance** a stronger conceptual category than a simple story of attempted destruction.

The most defensible surviving idea is therefore:

> Gandhi's assassination ended his life as an active political actor but created a long afterlife in which different political and social actors repeatedly preserved, appropriated, rejected, criticised, neutralised, or reinterpreted different parts of what Gandhi meant.

That is not the same claim as saying that one ideology has continuously tried to destroy Gandhi. The latter requires a much stronger historical-continuity case than the current evidence permits.

## 2. Attack on "An Ideology Tried to Kill Gandhi"

The sentence should be broken into five propositions and tested independently.

### Proposition 1 — Gandhi was assassinated because of ideological opposition

**Assessment: strongly supportable, but not as a single-cause explanation.**

Godse's own court statement presents the assassination as a political and ideological act. Contemporary reporting on his statement records that he justified the killing by reference to Gandhi's policies toward Muslims, Partition, and the perceived consequences for Hindus. Independent scholarship similarly places the assassination in the context of Hindu-nationalist hostility toward Gandhi and the acute political crisis of Partition. The historical record therefore supports treating ideology as a major motive.

But “ideological motive” should not be converted into “ideology was the sole cause.” The final act also belongs to an immediate political context of communal violence, Partition, disputes over Pakistan, fear, grievance, group networks, personal conviction, and strategic choice. A historian can accept that ideology mattered while rejecting monocausal language.

**Use for the book concept:** central evidence.

**Do not use it to prove:** that every person or institution in the wider ideological field shared responsibility for the murder.

### Proposition 2 — The relevant ideology can be clearly identified

**Assessment: not yet demonstrated without sharper terminology.**

The concept uses the singular phrase “an ideology,” but the historical field contains several non-equivalent categories:

- Hindutva;
- Hindu nationalism;
- Hindu Mahasabha politics;
- RSS ideology and institutional culture;
- Godse's individual ideological worldview;
- broader anti-Gandhian politics;
- and specific late-1940s positions on Partition, Pakistan, Muslims, and the meaning of nationhood.

These categories overlap, but they are not interchangeable. A book that alternates among them as though they are one actor will become unfalsifiable.

**Use for the book concept:** absolutely central. This is a terminology problem that must be solved before the thesis can be tested.

### Proposition 3 — Godse's ideology represents a larger ideological current

**Assessment: supportable at the level of ideological context; not automatically representative of every organisation.**

The available scholarship places Godse within a broader Hindu-nationalist political environment and documents his organisational and personal connections. Vinay Lal's account stresses his association with the Hindu Mahasabha and his earlier RSS involvement; later scholarship also argues that his organisational history and relationships cannot be reduced to the simplified claim that he was either wholly inside or wholly outside one organisation at the moment of the assassination. The current evidence therefore supports contextualising Godse within a wider ideological field.

However, “belongs to a wider ideological current” is different from “represents the entire movement.” The book must distinguish:

**influence → association → membership → network participation → conspiracy → organisational responsibility.**

Those are different historical claims with different evidentiary burdens.

**Use for the book concept:** central, but only with precise actor-level attribution.

### Proposition 4 — The assassination represented that broader ideology

**Assessment: plausible and potentially strong, but mechanism matters.**

The ideological content of Godse's rationale, the political context, and the documented hostility toward Gandhi make it reasonable to interpret the assassination as an expression of a particular anti-Gandhian political worldview. The claim becomes stronger if the book demonstrates how specific ideas moved from texts, speeches, organisations, relationships, and grievances into the decision to kill.

The dangerous shortcut is:

> Gandhi was opposed by Hindu nationalists → Godse was a Hindu nationalist → therefore “Hindu nationalism killed Gandhi.”

That formulation suppresses differences among thinkers and organisations and bypasses the actual network of people who planned and executed the crime.

A more defensible causal ladder is:

**ideas → political environment → interpersonal and organisational networks → conspiratorial decisions → operational act.**

The ideology can then be analysed as a **causal environment and source of justification**, rather than a literal historical actor.

**Use for the book concept:** central if the book follows the causal ladder.

### Proposition 5 — It is legitimate to treat “an ideology” as the historical actor

**Assessment: this is the greatest conceptual danger.**

Ideologies do not physically assassinate people. Individuals and groups do. An ideology can shape the categories through which actors understand grievances, enemies, duty, legitimacy, and violence, but the book must not use an abstract category to erase the agency of named conspirators.

This is especially important for Savarkar and the RSS. Savarkar was charged and acquitted in the Gandhi murder trial; the legal outcome should not be silently converted into either a finding of innocence on every historical question or a finding of criminal responsibility where the court did not establish it. Likewise, evidence of RSS association, organisational proximity, ideological influence, or later institutional memory should not automatically be rendered as formal institutional responsibility for the murder.

**Use for the book concept:** use the ideological category as context, not as a substitute for causal attribution.

### Godse, Partition, psychology, and immediate grievances

The book should treat these as interacting rather than mutually exclusive explanations.

Godse's stated motives were political and ideological, but the historical question is larger than his retrospective self-description. Partition, communal violence, disputes over Pakistan, perceptions of Muslim appeasement, and fear about the future of the Indian state formed the immediate political environment. Individual psychology and personal commitment matter too, but the evidence available here does not justify a speculative psychological diagnosis.

**Research-use rule:** do not add psychological claims unless primary evidence or serious scholarship supports them. Political grievance and ideological conviction are much safer analytical categories.

### Savarkar and RSS

The book should explicitly separate three questions:

1. What ideological influence did Savarkar have on Godse and the wider field?
2. What organisational relationships did Godse maintain with RSS and Hindu Mahasabha structures?
3. What evidence establishes or does not establish responsibility for the assassination itself?

The answers may be different. That distinction is essential.

## 3. Attack on "The Assassination Failed to Kill What Gandhi Represented"

This proposition cannot be evaluated until “what Gandhi represented” is decomposed.

### Gandhi's image

**Assessment: strongest case for survival.**

Gandhi's face, name, memorials, ceremonies, public symbolism, currency imagery, and international recognition clearly survived. His death did not end public memory. The persistence of this image is not in serious doubt.

But image survival proves **symbolic survival**, not survival of the entire political programme.

### Gandhi's political philosophy

**Assessment: partial survival, major transformation.**

Key commitments associated with Gandhi — nonviolence, religious pluralism, moral politics, swadeshi, village-centred economics, trusteeship, constructive work, and suspicion of industrial modernity — did not survive as one intact governing programme of the Indian republic. Some ideas retained major influence; others were marginalised, transformed, or selectively detached from their original context.

Therefore the phrase “failed to kill what he represented” is too broad unless the book specifies which parts survived.

### Gandhi's moral authority

**Assessment: strong survival, but selective and contested.**

Gandhi remains a moral reference point for political actors across ideological lines. This creates a major counterexample to a simple destruction thesis: people can reject important parts of Gandhi while still appealing to his moral prestige.

That means **appropriation and neutralisation** may sometimes explain Gandhi's posthumous power better than either preservation or destruction.

### Gandhian political practice

**Assessment: weak survival as a dominant national operating model.**

Satyagraha, constructive work, voluntary poverty, decentralised social organisation, and a fully Gandhian political economy did not become the dominant institutional model of post-independence India. The book should not infer survival of practice merely from survival of rhetoric.

### Gandhi as a national symbol

**Assessment: extremely strong survival.**

This is likely the safest version of the “failed to kill” argument. Gandhi became embedded in national memory, and the state has repeatedly participated in the construction and maintenance of this symbolic afterlife.

### Gandhi's institutional influence

**Assessment: mixed and domain-specific.**

Gandhian ideas have influenced activism, civil-rights traditions, debates on violence and peace, social movements, and public rhetoric. But influence is not equivalent to institutional dominance. The book should measure influence by sector and period rather than making a totalising claim.

### Core adversarial conclusion

The original sentence wrongly bundles together six different survival questions.

The most useful distinction is:

**Gandhi the person → Gandhi's political philosophy → Gandhi's moral authority → Gandhian political practice → Gandhi as national symbol → institutional afterlife.**

A strong book can argue that death transformed Gandhi into a more malleable symbol. It cannot simply infer that because the symbol survived, the programme survived intact.

## 4. Attack on "The Struggle Continues"

The phrase “still trying to destroy his legacy” has an unusually high evidentiary burden.

The repository instructions require Phase 2 research to distinguish criticism, competing interpretations, and what the evidence does and does not establish. No completed Claude packet is presently available to do that comparison. Independent scholarship nevertheless shows that the post-1948 history is more complicated than a continuous attack narrative.

### Distinguish the following processes

| Process | What it means | Does it prove a continuous anti-Gandhi project? |
|---|---|---|
| Criticism | Arguing that Gandhi's ideas or actions were wrong | No |
| Rejection | Refusing Gandhi's political or moral framework | No |
| Reinterpretation | Assigning Gandhi a different meaning | No |
| Appropriation | Adopting selected Gandhi symbols or ideas for another project | No |
| Symbolic use | Invoking Gandhi for legitimacy or rhetoric | No |
| Neutralisation | Converting a disruptive political thinker into a safe national icon | Not by itself |
| Deliberate discrediting | Intentionally seeking to reduce Gandhi's authority | Potentially, but requires actor-specific evidence |
| Godse glorification | Celebrating or rehabilitating Gandhi's assassin | Evidence of a specific counter-memory, not automatically proof of organisational continuity |

Modern scholarship on the RSS and Hindu nationalism is especially important because it contains an apparent contradiction to the original thesis: actors who have been strongly critical of Gandhi have also attempted to appropriate parts of Gandhi's legacy. This is evidence that the struggle is not simply **Gandhi versus anti-Gandhi**. It can be a struggle over **which Gandhi is politically useful**.

That is analytically more interesting and more defensible.

### What would have to be proven for “still trying to destroy” to survive

For a modern example to qualify as continuity rather than analogy or recurrence, the book should demonstrate, where applicable:

1. organisational continuity or demonstrable institutional inheritance;
2. ideological continuity on the specific Gandhi-related issue;
3. transmission of ideas, texts, personnel, or strategic memory;
4. evidence of intentional continuity;
5. a materially similar objective concerning Gandhi's legacy.

A modern anti-Gandhi statement that lacks these links is evidence of **current criticism**, not proof of a seven-decade project.

### Core adversarial conclusion

“Struggle continues” is defensible only if “struggle” is defined as **contestation over Gandhi's meaning**. It is not yet defensible as a claim that one ideological actor has maintained an uninterrupted campaign to destroy Gandhi.

## 5. Problems With the Word "Ideology"

The singular noun creates four risks.

### Risk 1 — It hides heterogeneity

Hindutva, Hindu nationalism, Hindu Mahasabha politics, RSS organisational culture, and Godse's worldview overlap but developed through distinct texts, institutions, and historical periods.

### Risk 2 — It encourages retrospective projection

A present-day ideological category can be projected backward onto 1948 without proving that people in 1948 used the category in the same way or pursued the same objective.

### Risk 3 — It blurs ideology and organisation

An organisation may adopt, reinterpret, narrow, or strategically conceal elements of an ideological tradition. An individual may borrow from multiple political traditions. The book should not treat “ideology” as synonymous with membership.

### Risk 4 — It makes the thesis difficult to falsify

If every anti-Gandhi position is labelled the same ideology, every later criticism can be treated as confirming evidence. That is circular reasoning.

### Recommended terminology hierarchy

**Use narrowly:**

- Godse's worldview;
- Hindu Mahasabha ideology or political position;
- RSS ideology or organisational position;
- Hindutva as a defined intellectual tradition;
- Hindu nationalism as a broader political field;
- anti-Gandhian politics as a broad descriptive category.

**Avoid:** using “the ideology” without naming the object of analysis.

## 6. Problems With Historical Continuity

The proposed bridge is:

**1948 → later decades → present.**

The danger is treating elapsed time as evidence of continuity.

### Actor continuity

The people involved in 1948 are mostly not the people involved in later controversies. A later generation must therefore inherit ideas through institutions, texts, organisations, memory, or explicit political transmission. That inheritance must be documented.

### Organisational continuity

Organisations evolve. Leadership, strategies, legal conditions, political goals, alliances, and public presentation change. A later organisation may emerge from an older network while still departing substantially from earlier positions.

### Ideological continuity

Even where the same label survives, the substance may change. The book needs issue-specific comparison rather than label-based continuity.

### Intentional continuity

This is the hardest test. A present-day critic may independently conclude that Gandhi's ideas are wrong. That does not mean they are intentionally continuing Godse's project.

### Gandhi's changing meaning

The target itself changes. Gandhi in 1948 was a living political actor intervening in communal violence and the new state's moral crisis. Gandhi in later decades becomes a memorial figure, classroom figure, development symbol, global peace icon, political resource, and object of historiographical debate.

Therefore the same word — “Gandhi” — can refer to different historical objects.

### Presentism risk

The book must not use current political conflict as retroactive proof of the meaning of the 1948 assassination. The present can raise questions about the past, but it cannot silently settle past causation.

### Continuity test for each modern case

For each modern example, the evidence should be tagged as:

- **continuity** if a documented chain is established;
- **inheritance** if ideas or institutional memory are demonstrably transmitted;
- **recurrence** if a similar position reappears independently;
- **appropriation** if Gandhi is selectively incorporated into a different project;
- **contestation** if actors simply fight over his meaning;
- **analogy** if the link is interpretive rather than historical.

If the book uses these labels consistently, its argument becomes much harder to attack.

## 7. Counterexamples to the Original Narrative

The strongest adversarial move is to seek evidence that would be difficult to explain under the original thesis.

### Counterexample 1 — State memorialisation

Gandhi became deeply embedded in the public memory of the Indian state. Scholarship on the aftermath of the assassination treats the creation of national memory and the political uses of Gandhi as historically important. This makes a simple “failed to kill his legacy” statement misleading if it implies the state or mainstream political culture left Gandhi politically abandoned.

**Conceptual use:** proves symbolic survival, not survival of every Gandhian idea.

### Counterexample 2 — Political actors can appropriate Gandhi while disagreeing with him

Recent scholarship on Gandhi and the RSS specifically argues that Hindu nationalists have both criticised Gandhi and attempted to appropriate elements of his image and legacy.

**Conceptual use:** very strong counterexample to a destruction-only model.

### Counterexample 3 — Gandhi has legitimate critics outside the alleged continuity chain

Ambedkar's critique of Gandhi is not reducible to Hindu nationalist hostility. Nor are scholarly debates about caste, race, gender, sexuality, development, or political coercion. These criticisms can be historically serious without implying any desire to erase Gandhi.

**Conceptual use:** prevents the book from classifying every criticism as ideological warfare.

### Counterexample 4 — Gandhi's legacy itself is unstable

The research architecture explicitly includes historiography and legacy because different scholars and political actors interpret Gandhi differently. This means the historical object is not a single fixed legacy that one side either preserves or destroys.

**Conceptual use:** supports “contested inheritance” over “single legacy under attack.”

### Counterexample 5 — Godse and the assassination became their own objects of memory

The post-assassination history includes not only Gandhi commemoration but also later attempts to frame Godse, the assassination, and the political meaning of the event. That creates a second memory struggle layered onto Gandhi's own legacy.

**Conceptual use:** the book may be stronger if it studies competing memories of the assassination, not only Gandhi's survival.

## 8. Gandhi's Flaws That Complicate the Story

The book does not need to attack Gandhi. It does need to prevent a heroic baseline from distorting the historical question.

### Caste and varna

Gandhi's campaigns against untouchability coexist with major tensions over caste and varna. Ambedkar's disagreements with Gandhi therefore matter to the book's concept because they demonstrate that opposition to Gandhi did not originate solely from the political current implicated in his assassination.

**Conceptual use:** strong counterexample to any binary in which Gandhi's opponents are treated as one camp.

### Poona Pact

The 1932 confrontation over separate electorates and the Poona Pact raises questions about moral pressure, political representation, and who had authority to define reform. The book should not reduce this dispute to a slogan; it should use it as evidence that Gandhi's moral authority was itself contested by major political actors before 1948.

**Conceptual use:** strengthens the distinction between Gandhi's moral prestige and universal moral agreement.

### South Africa

Gandhi's early political activity in South Africa contains racial and hierarchical assumptions that complicate a retrospective image of an already-finished moral figure. The book should not project the later Gandhi backward without qualification.

**Conceptual use:** demonstrates that “Gandhi's legacy” has always required interpretation.

### Political methods and fasting

A critic can reasonably interpret fasting and moral pressure as forms of political coercion even when Gandhi understood them as self-suffering. The book need not endorse that interpretation; it should recognise that moral authority can be experienced as coercive.

**Conceptual use:** useful for explaining why people could reject Gandhi without seeking to destroy his memory.

### Communal politics and political failure

Gandhi's commitment to Hindu-Muslim unity is central to the final period, but his success or failure must be judged historically rather than assumed from intention alone. His failure to prevent Partition or communal violence complicates simplistic ideas of moral invulnerability.

### Brahmacharya and personal conduct

The brahmacharya experiments create a further challenge to any universalised saintly image. Their relevance is not to sensationalise Gandhi, but to show why a posthumous symbolic Gandhi can become detached from a historically complicated person.

### Overall adversarial conclusion

Acknowledging Gandhi's flaws **weakens a simplistic heroic story but can strengthen the book's deeper argument about contested legacy**. A symbol becomes politically powerful partly because different generations can choose which elements of a complicated person to remember.

## 9. Problems With Godse's Stated Motive

Godse's own statement is indispensable and insufficient.

### What it safely establishes

His public defence demonstrates that he wanted the assassination to be understood as a reasoned political act. Contemporary reporting of his courtroom statement records explicit arguments about Gandhi's policies, Muslims, Partition, and perceived Hindu interests.

### What it does not safely establish

It does not by itself establish the full history of the conspiracy, the complete network of influence, or the responsibility of every person or organisation later connected to the case.

### Self-presentation problem

Godse had obvious reasons to frame the act in a particular moral and political language. His statement is therefore both evidence of motive and an intentional act of political communication.

### Multiple motives can coexist

There is no need to choose between:

- ideology;
- immediate political grievance;
- communal context;
- personal conviction;
- strategic calculation;
- and group identity.

All can operate together.

### Savarkar problem

Savarkar's legal acquittal means the book must distinguish:

- ideological relationship;
- political influence;
- association;
- evidence considered by investigators or later scholars;
- and criminal responsibility established in court.

The book should not convert these categories into one another.

### RSS problem

The historical dispute over Godse's relationship with the RSS should be presented as a historiographical question with evidence on multiple sides, not settled by a slogan. Scholarship does support a close relationship between Godse and the wider Hindu-nationalist field; the exact organisational status and the distinction between influence and institutional responsibility require precise sourcing.

### Research-use rule

Godse's statement should be treated as **primary evidence of what Godse wanted to say about his act**, not as a complete explanation of why the assassination occurred.

Triangulation should use:

- trial records;
- police and government records;
- correspondence;
- organisational records;
- witness evidence;
- contemporary reporting;
- later inquiry material;
- and serious historiography.

## 10. Strongest Alternative Interpretation

The strongest alternative is not that the original concept is wholly false. It is that the project has identified the wrong unit of continuity.

A stronger interpretation would be:

> **The assassination ended Gandhi's political life but transformed the problem of Gandhi's meaning. After 1948, different actors repeatedly fought over which Gandhi should survive: the political thinker, the moral authority, the national symbol, the religious pluralist, the critic of modernity, the apostle of nonviolence, the object of caste criticism, the global peace icon, or a selectively appropriated political resource. The historical struggle is therefore better understood as contested inheritance than as one ideology's uninterrupted attempt to destroy a single legacy.**

### Why this survives better

1. It keeps the assassination central without making it the cause of every later development.
2. It allows documented ideological continuity where evidence genuinely exists.
3. It also allows discontinuity where organisations, actors, and goals changed.
4. It explains why Gandhi can be simultaneously commemorated and criticised.
5. It makes Gandhi's contradictions part of the evidence rather than a problem the book has to hide.
6. It is falsifiable: specific continuity claims can be tested case by case.

### What is lost

The original concept has greater rhetorical force because it offers a single antagonist and a single historical arc. The alternative is less simple. That is precisely why it is safer historically.

## 11. Five Strongest Arguments Against the Current Concept

### 1. The central actor is undefined

“The ideology” is too vague to support a historical argument. A thesis cannot be tested if the independent variable changes between Godse's worldview, Hindu nationalism, Hindutva, RSS, and Hindu Mahasabha politics.

### 2. The concept risks converting association into responsibility

Godse's relationships with organisations and thinkers are historically important, but association, influence, membership, conspiracy, and institutional responsibility are not interchangeable.

### 3. The continuity claim is much stronger than the available evidence

The move from 1948 to the present requires a documented chain of transmission. Repeated criticism is not enough.

### 4. Symbolic survival is being confused with survival of Gandhian politics

Gandhi's face and name survived. That does not establish that his economic programme, political methods, social philosophy, or institutional preferences survived intact.

### 5. The thesis cannot explain its own counterexamples

State commemoration, selective political appropriation, legitimate criticism from non-Hindu-nationalist actors, and changing interpretations of Gandhi all fit a contested-memory model better than a destruction-only model.

## 12. What Survives the Attack

### Survives strongly

- The assassination had a major ideological component.
- Godse must be analysed as part of a wider political and ideological environment.
- Gandhi's death did not end his historical significance.
- Gandhi's posthumous meaning has been repeatedly contested.
- Gandhi's image and Gandhi's political programme are not the same historical object.

### Survives with narrowing

- “An ideology tried to kill Gandhi” can survive only if the ideology is named and the causal chain is reconstructed.
- “The assassination failed to kill what Gandhi represented” can survive if the object is defined as symbolic and moral afterlife rather than total ideological survival.
- “The struggle continues” can survive as a claim about contestation over Gandhi's meaning.

### Does not yet survive

- “The same ideology has continuously tried to destroy Gandhi's legacy” from 1948 to the present.
- Any automatic equation of Godse with every organisation or thinker associated with Hindu nationalism.
- Any inference from symbolic commemoration to survival of Gandhi's full philosophy.

## 13. What Must Change

### Change the central noun

Replace **“the ideology”** with the specific ideological or organisational actor relevant to each historical case.

### Change the central verb

Replace **“destroy his legacy”** with measurable processes:

- criticise;
- delegitimise;
- reinterpret;
- appropriate;
- neutralise;
- marginalise;
- glorify the assassin;
- or contest.

### Make the survival test explicit

For each Gandhi-related claim, identify whether the evidence concerns:

1. image;
2. philosophy;
3. moral authority;
4. political practice;
5. national symbolism;
6. institutional influence.

### Change the continuity standard

No modern example should be called “continuity” unless the book can document transmission rather than similarity.

### Preserve disagreement

Phase 3 explicitly requires contradictions and disagreements to be preserved rather than flattened. The missing Claude packets must eventually be compared, not merely merged into one narrative.

## 15. Handoff to Product Manager

### Single biggest weakness in the current concept

**It treats a heterogeneous historical field as one continuous ideological actor.** The original sentence collapses individual motive, ideological environment, organisational association, conspiracy, post-1948 political development, and modern memory into one causal story.

### Single strongest surviving idea

**Gandhi's assassination transformed rather than ended the historical struggle over Gandhi's meaning.** The stronger book question is what different actors did with Gandhi after death.

### Most dangerous unsupported claim

**“Still trying to destroy his legacy.”** This is the proposition most likely to fail under hostile historical review because it requires continuity of ideology, organisation, transmission, intention, and objective across decades.

### Most important conceptual distinction

**Gandhi's image is not Gandhi's political philosophy.** Symbolic survival, moral authority, political practice, and institutional influence must be analysed separately.

### Strongest alternative framing

> **The assassination ended Gandhi's political life but created a contested afterlife. Different actors have since preserved, criticised, appropriated, neutralised, reinterpreted, or rejected different Gandhis. The historical question is therefore not simply whether an ideology failed to destroy Gandhi, but which parts of Gandhi survived, in what form, and who gained the power to define them.**

### Evidence that would determine whether the original framing can be recovered

The decisive research is a documented chain connecting:

**Godse's ideology → the assassination network → post-1948 organisational and ideological development → later actors → specific modern attempts to reshape Gandhi's legacy.**

If that chain demonstrates genuine transmission and intentional continuity, selected portions of the original thesis may be defensible after narrowing the actors and time periods. If it does not, the book should pivot to contested memory, appropriation, transformation, and competing political inheritances.

### Final Phase 3 judgement

**Do not approve the original wording as the working thesis.** Preserve the assassination/legacy connection as the central research problem, but downgrade “one ideology continuously trying to destroy Gandhi” from thesis to **hypothesis requiring proof**.

The next Product Manager stage should not ask, “How do we make the original thesis stronger?” It should ask, **“What evidence would be required to make the continuity claim true, and what evidence would falsify it?”**

2. “Hindutva in the shadow of the Mahatma: M. S. Golwalkar, M. K. Gandhi, and the RSS in post-colonial India,” *Modern Asian Studies* — evidence relevant to post-1948 appropriation, criticism, Godse, and the RSS's changing relationship to Gandhi.
   https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/modern-asian-studies/article/hindutva-in-the-shadow-of-the-mahatma-m-s-gandhi-and-the-rss-in-post-colonial-india/68676BDD205B98828EC18C53C20BE4A7

3. “Performing Peace: Gandhi's assassination as a critical moment in the consolidation of the Nehruvian state,” *Modern Asian Studies* — useful for the immediate aftermath, state response, and construction of post-assassination political memory.
   https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/modern-asian-studies/article/performing-peace-gandhis-assassination-as-a-critical-moment-in-the-consolidation-of-the-nehruvian-state/B13C16345C0092DF55010CDEFA9ACACF

4. Contemporary report of Godse's courtroom statement — useful as evidence of Godse's own self-presentation and stated political rationale, not as a complete causal explanation.
   https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/AG19481109.2.42

5. Cambridge material on Gandhi in South Africa — useful for testing retrospective saintly framing and racial assumptions in Gandhi's early political career.
   https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/8F7830A532D7944B2D00C6CC56850465/S0022278X00018590a.pdf/gandhi_in_south_africa.pdf

6. Government of India / Ambedkar material — useful for the political-constitutional context of Ambedkar's disagreements and representation questions relevant to the Poona Pact and Gandhi's contested moral authority.
   https://www.mea.gov.in/Images/CPV/Volume9.pdf
