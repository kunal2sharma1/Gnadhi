# GPT Worker 1 — Thesis Options and Concept Architecture

## 1. Executive Summary

The verified research does not support freezing the original formulation — “An ideology tried to kill Gandhi, but failed to kill what he represented, and is still trying to destroy his legacy” — as a literal historical thesis.

It does, however, strongly support the **historical intuition underneath it**: Gandhi was assassinated in a political and ideological conflict; his death did not end his public or moral significance; and the meaning of Gandhi became, and remains, a contested political and intellectual field.

The research makes three major corrections necessary.

First, the phrase **“an ideology tried to kill Gandhi”** is too abstract. The primary record supports a planned assassination in which Nathuram Godse articulated ideological and political motives, operating within a wider Hindu-nationalist/Hindu-Sanghatanist political environment. It does not justify treating an ideology as a single acting organisation, nor does it establish blanket institutional responsibility for every organisation associated with the conspirators.

Second, **“what Gandhi represented” must be disaggregated**. Gandhi's image and national-symbol status survived extremely strongly. His moral authority survived, but in contested and selective form. His political philosophy survived partially and unevenly. His political practice did not survive as an intact national governing programme. The evidence therefore supports symbolic and contested survival more strongly than wholesale philosophical survival.

Third, **“still trying to destroy his legacy” is the weakest part of the original concept**. The research verifies continuing conflict over Gandhi's meaning, including criticism, rejection, appropriation, selective representation, possible neutralisation, counter-memory and fringe Godse glorification. It does not establish one continuous ideological campaign, with uninterrupted organisational or ideological continuity from the 1948 assassination to present-day actors.

The concept should therefore evolve from **continuous destruction** toward **contested inheritance / contested meaning**, while preserving the original project's central dramatic question: what happens to a political and moral legacy when the person carrying it is killed?

Five genuinely distinct concepts are viable:

1. **Assassination → Afterlife:** Gandhi's assassination transformed the political meaning of Gandhi and began a long afterlife of contestation.
2. **Symbol vs Programme:** What exactly survived after Gandhi's death — image, moral authority, philosophy, practice, or national symbol?
3. **Contested Inheritance:** Different actors inherited different Gandhis, accepting some elements while rejecting, appropriating or neutralising others.
4. **Limits of Assassination:** Can killing a political actor destroy the political meaning associated with that actor? Gandhi provides a historically testable case.
5. **Rebuilt Original:** Gandhi was killed, but not erased; the surviving legacy became the object of recurring struggles over meaning rather than one uninterrupted campaign of destruction.

On the evidence currently available, **Option C — Contested Inheritance** is the strongest overall concept, with **Option A — Assassination → Afterlife** a very close second. Option E is the strongest preservation of the author's original intuition but remains more vulnerable to overreading modern conflicts. Option D has the greatest theoretical originality but requires more comparative framing to sustain a full historical book. Option B is conceptually rigorous and resistant to simplistic political readings, but risks becoming more analytical than dramatic unless structured through concrete historical episodes.

No option should yet be treated as the final thesis. The purpose of this document is to identify the strongest surviving architectures for the next stage of testing.

## 2. What the Original Idea Is Really Trying to Investigate

The project materials show that the original idea is fundamentally organised around a historical paradox:

**Gandhi was physically destroyed, but something associated with Gandhi survived — and the struggle over that surviving meaning did not end with his death.**

The author's central interest is therefore not merely the assassination as an isolated crime. The assassination is the hinge between two questions:

1. **Why was Gandhi killed?**
2. **What happened to the political and symbolic object that the killers were attacking after Gandhi was dead?**

The research shows that the first question is historically rich because Godse's own primary statements make clear that he understood his action in ideological, political and strategic terms. The assassination was not presented by him as random personal revenge. The criminal record also establishes a wider conspiracy and the January 20 bombing as part of the same criminal history.

The second question is even more fertile for the book because the research shows that Gandhi's posthumous life did not follow a simple success/failure pattern. Gandhi remained extraordinarily visible. At the same time, different actors gave him radically different meanings.

The project is therefore best understood as an investigation of the relationship among:

**assassination → political meaning → memory → inheritance → appropriation/rejection → contested legacy.**

That is broader and historically stronger than a simple narrative of “Gandhi versus the ideology that killed him.”

The emotional/intellectual question appears to be:

> **Can the death of a political figure end the political conflict represented by that figure, or can the killing transform the figure into a symbol that becomes even more contested afterward?**

That question preserves the dramatic force of the original idea without requiring an unsupported claim of continuous organisational conspiracy.

The strongest evidence currently available supports:

- Godse's ideological/political self-understanding;
- the existence of a wider criminal conspiracy;
- Gandhi's highly active final political period;
- the extraordinary endurance of Gandhi's public image;
- and the continuing contest over what Gandhi means.

The weakest part is the claim of one continuous historical project “still trying” to destroy Gandhi's legacy.

What should survive even if the wording changes completely is the **paradox of survival after assassination**:

> Gandhi's body could be killed; the political meaning attached to Gandhi could not simply be killed with it.

That is a hypothesis supported enough to investigate, but it still requires careful definition of what “political meaning” includes.

## 3. Strengths of the Original Concept

### 3.1 It contains a strong historical hinge

The assassination provides a clean chronological dividing line. Before 30 January 1948, Gandhi was an active political actor. After 30 January 1948, Gandhi became a posthumous political, moral and symbolic object.

That makes the assassination structurally important rather than merely dramatic.

### 3.2 It connects biography to political history

The concept allows the book to move from an individual assassination into questions of political authority, ideological conflict, memory and national identity.

### 3.3 The first clause is substantially defensible

The primary-source verification establishes that Godse himself described his actions in explicitly political and ideological terms. His statements identify Hindu Sanghatanist/Hindu-nationalist convictions, Partition, Gandhi's communal politics, the January 1948 fast, the Pakistan payment and Gandhi's perceived political authority as major components of his reasoning.

The safest conclusion is not that ideology alone caused the assassination, but that **ideological conviction was a major component of Godse's stated motive within a broader political and conspiratorial context**.

### 3.4 The survival intuition is strongly supported at the symbolic level

Gandhi remained deeply embedded in public and state memory after 1948. His national-symbol status survived across political changes. The evidence does not support erasure as a general description of the post-1948 experience.

### 3.5 The concept asks a question that can sustain multiple historical periods

The assassination can lead into immediate state response, mourning, commemoration, later criticism, political appropriation, Godse counter-memory, textbook disputes, heritage disputes, scholarly reinterpretation and contemporary political uses.

The danger is not lack of material. The danger is falsely treating all that material as one movement.

## 4. Weaknesses of the Original Concept

### 4.1 “An ideology” falsely compresses levels of analysis

The evidence supports an ideological environment and Godse's individual ideological reasoning. It does not support treating “an ideology” as a single organisational actor that can be assigned one continuous intention.

The causal chain should be:

**ideas → political identity/grievance → relationships/networks → conspiratorial decisions → operational act.**

### 4.2 “They” is historically ambiguous

If “they” means Godse, Apte and other conspirators, that is a concrete historical category. If “they” means RSS, Hindu Mahasabha, Savarkar, Hindu nationalism, or a modern political formation collectively, the evidence varies dramatically and must be specified.

### 4.3 “What Gandhi represented” is too broad to falsify

The research demonstrates that several different things survived to different degrees:

- image: very strongly;
- national symbolism: extremely strongly;
- moral authority: strongly but selectively;
- political philosophy: partially and unevenly;
- political practice: less strongly as a dominant national model;
- institutional influence: mixed.

A thesis that treats all six as one object will obscure rather than explain the evidence.

### 4.4 “Still trying to destroy his legacy” overstates present-day continuity

The strongest post-1948 evidence demonstrates contested inheritance. It does not demonstrate a single uninterrupted campaign.

The research explicitly distinguishes:

- criticism;
- rejection;
- reinterpretation;
- appropriation;
- symbolic use;
- neutralisation;
- counter-memory;
- deliberate discrediting.

These categories cannot be collapsed without case-specific evidence.

### 4.5 The original formulation risks political presentism

The temptation is to find modern anti-Gandhi rhetoric and treat it as proof of a historical line extending back to 1948. The research says that resemblance is not enough. Continuity requires evidence of transmission, personnel, organisations, texts, intentions, or demonstrable inheritance.

### 4.6 It risks making the entire book answer a proposition the evidence does not require

A strong book does not need to prove that a continuous organisation “still” wants Gandhi destroyed if the deeper historical question is more interesting: **what happened when Gandhi became a contested symbol after his assassination?**

## 6. Thesis Option A

### Thesis / Central Proposition

**Assassination → Afterlife:** Gandhi's assassination did not conclude the political history of Gandhi; it transformed it. The killing ended Gandhi's life as an active political actor, but it created a posthumous figure whose meaning became a continuing field of political and intellectual contest. The book would trace how the assassination, the immediate political response, national commemoration, competing interpretations, appropriation, criticism and counter-memory changed the question from “What was Gandhi doing?” to “What does Gandhi mean now?”

### Core Historical Claim

The assassination should be understood not only as the violent end of Gandhi's political life but as the beginning of a distinct posthumous political afterlife. The killing produced a symbolic Gandhi whose meaning could be used, contested, reinterpreted and selectively inherited by actors across later decades.

### Main Research Question

**How did Gandhi's assassination transform Gandhi from a living political actor into a contested posthumous political symbol?**

### What Makes It Different

This option makes **chronological transformation** the book's central engine. It is less about proving what the killer represented and more about showing what happened to Gandhi as a political object after the killing.

### Evidence Supporting It

- The assassination itself is strongly established in the criminal and judicial record.
- Godse's primary statements show that the killing was understood by him as a political and ideological intervention.
- The assassination immediately became a national political and emotional event, not merely a criminal proceeding.
- The research on post-1948 memory shows durable state commemoration, continuing political appropriation, criticism, counter-memory and scholarly reassessment.
- The research strongly supports continuing contest over Gandhi's meaning.

### Evidence That Complicates It

- The post-1948 field is not one struggle. It contains incompatible traditions of criticism, appropriation, rejection and commemoration.
- Gandhi's symbolic survival was much stronger than survival of his entire political programme.
- Modern manifestations should not automatically be traced back to the assassination ideology.

### Biggest Vulnerability

The concept could become a general history of Gandhi's memory without a sufficiently sharp causal argument. The book would need to show **how and why the assassination changed Gandhi's political afterlife**, rather than merely listing later uses of Gandhi.

### What Additional Research Is Needed

- A tighter chronology of immediate post-assassination state and public responses.
- Key turning points in Gandhi's posthumous political meaning.
- Case studies showing changes in Gandhi's public interpretation across decades.
- Stronger evidence on when and how Godse counter-memory emerged and changed.
- Comparative examination of appropriation, rejection and commemoration rather than only anti-Gandhi examples.

### Potential Book Structure

**Part I:** Gandhi as living political actor → assassination and immediate record.

**Part II:** The transformation from living leader to national symbol.

**Part III:** Competing inheritances across political and intellectual traditions.

**Part IV:** Appropriation, rejection, counter-memory and neutralisation.

**Part V:** What the contested afterlife says about the political power and limits of Gandhi's legacy.

## 7. Thesis Option B

### Thesis / Central Proposition

**Symbol vs Political Programme:** The central historical puzzle is not simply whether Gandhi survived after death, but **which Gandhi survived**. His image and national symbolism became remarkably durable, while his political philosophy and practical programme survived unevenly and often selectively. The book would separate Gandhi the icon from Gandhi the political thinker and ask whether the survival of the symbol should be mistaken for the survival of the politics.

### Core Historical Claim

The strongest evidence of Gandhi's posthumous survival concerns his image, moral authority and national-symbol status. His complete political programme did not survive in the same form. The post-1948 history is therefore a story of **symbolic endurance combined with political transformation and selective inheritance**.

### Main Research Question

**What exactly survived when Gandhi survived — the man, the moral symbol, the political philosophy, the political practice, or only selected elements of each?**

### What Makes It Different

This is a **conceptual deconstruction** of “legacy.” Rather than asking whether Gandhi survived, it asks what survival means.

### Evidence Supporting It

- Durable national commemoration is strongly established.
- Gandhi remains a major moral and symbolic reference point.
- Scholarship documents selective political appropriation.
- Gandhi's final 1948 programme itself was more radical and institutionally demanding than the simplified public symbol often retained.
- The 29 January Lok Sevak Sangh draft shows Gandhi was still trying to transform political practice, not merely preserve ceremonial status.

### Evidence That Complicates It

- Symbol and substance cannot always be cleanly separated; moral imagery can influence political action even when the full programme is not adopted.
- Different institutions and actors may genuinely believe they are preserving Gandhi even while altering his political content.
- Claims of “neutralisation” require case-specific proof of what was omitted and why.

### Biggest Vulnerability

It may become too abstract or academic unless anchored in concrete historical episodes. Readers may understand the distinction intellectually without feeling the historical stakes.

### What Additional Research Is Needed

- Systematic comparison of Gandhi's own final political priorities with later public representations.
- Case studies of state commemoration, political speeches, civic programmes, social movements and educational representations.
- Evidence showing where symbolic Gandhi diverges materially from political Gandhi.
- Research identifying cases of genuine substantive Gandhian inheritance, not only dilution.

### Potential Book Structure

**Part I:** Gandhi's final political programme as documented in 1947–48.

**Part II:** The creation of Gandhi the national symbol after assassination.

**Part III:** Image versus philosophy.

**Part IV:** Appropriation and selective inheritance across competing political traditions.

**Part V:** Which parts of Gandhi survived, which were transformed, and which effectively disappeared from mainstream political practice.

## 8. Thesis Option C

### Thesis / Central Proposition

**Contested Inheritance:** Gandhi's death did not create one successor to his political legacy. It created a struggle over inheritance. Congress traditions, Ambedkarite critics, Hindu-nationalist actors, left critics, postcolonial scholars, social movements, state institutions and international admirers all inherited, rejected, appropriated or reinterpreted different parts of Gandhi. The historical problem is therefore not whether Gandhi “won” or “lost,” but **who claimed which Gandhi, why, and what happened to the meaning of the parts they selected**.

### Core Historical Claim

Gandhi's post-1948 legacy is best understood as **contested inheritance**: no single actor inherited the whole Gandhi. Different actors took different elements — nonviolence, nationalism, cleanliness, simplicity, village politics, Hindu-Muslim unity, moral authority, anti-colonial symbolism, or social reform — and placed them into competing political frameworks.

### Main Research Question

**Who inherited Gandhi after his assassination, which Gandhi did they inherit, which parts did they reject, and how did those selective inheritances reshape Gandhi's meaning?**

### What Makes It Different

This option is not principally about assassination aftermath or symbolic survival. Its central object is **competition over inheritance**.

### Evidence Supporting It

- Strong evidence that Gandhi's meaning remained contested after 1948.
- Evidence of criticism from ideologically independent traditions, including Ambedkarite, left, postcolonial and Hindu-nationalist currents.
- Evidence of selective political appropriation, including appropriation by actors who reject important parts of Gandhian politics.
- Evidence of fringe Godse counter-memory.
- Durable state commemoration alongside strong political and scholarly disagreement.
- Gandhi's final political agenda itself contained multiple components that could be separately inherited or rejected.

### Evidence That Complicates It

- “Inheritance” can be sincere rather than manipulative.
- Selective appropriation does not automatically equal hypocrisy or neutralisation.
- Not every critic or appropriator has any historical connection to the assassination ideology.
- The category must not imply all post-1948 conflict belongs to one lineage.

### Biggest Vulnerability

The book could become too sprawling. “Who inherited Gandhi?” potentially includes too many actors, countries, decades and interpretive traditions. It will need strict selection criteria.

### What Additional Research Is Needed

- A periodised map of major inheritance moments.
- A finite set of high-value case studies.
- Evidence for how actors themselves claimed Gandhi rather than how the author interprets them.
- Comparative analysis of genuine inheritance versus symbolic appropriation.
- Clear criteria distinguishing inheritance from mere invocation.

### Potential Book Structure

**Part I:** The Gandhi who died — final political position.

**Part II:** The birth of the posthumous Gandhi.

**Part III:** Competing inheritors and competing Gandhis.

**Part IV:** Criticism, appropriation, rejection, neutralisation and counter-memory.

**Part V:** What “Gandhi” ultimately became in the hands of different inheritors.

## 9. Thesis Option D

### Thesis / Central Proposition

**Ideology and the Limits of Assassination:** The book asks whether political assassination can actually destroy a political idea. Gandhi provides a historically unusual case in which a politically motivated killing ended the leader's life but failed to eliminate the symbolic and moral argument attached to him. The deeper subject is the political limits of assassination: what violence can destroy, what it can transform, and what it cannot control once a political figure becomes a symbol.

### Core Historical Claim

Assassination can eliminate a political actor's agency but cannot guarantee control over the meaning that survives after death. Gandhi's case demonstrates how killing can produce a martyr-symbol whose legacy becomes harder to control precisely because the dead person can no longer revise, negotiate or discipline his own public meaning.

### Main Research Question

**What can a political assassination actually destroy — a person, a movement, a political programme, a symbol, or none of these completely?**

### What Makes It Different

This option moves from Gandhi-specific history toward a **theory of political assassination**, using Gandhi as the central case rather than treating assassination only as a starting event for memory history.

### Evidence Supporting It

- Gandhi's assassination is firmly documented.
- Godse's own stated motive makes it clear that he believed removing Gandhi could alter India's political direction.
- Gandhi's symbolic survival is exceptionally strong.
- His meaning became more contested after death rather than disappearing.
- The existence of later appropriation, criticism and counter-memory shows the assassin could not determine the posthumous meaning of Gandhi.

### Evidence That Complicates It

- It cannot be proved from the current record that Godse's goal was to destroy all forms of Gandhi's legacy rather than alter immediate political policy by removing a powerful actor.
- Symbolic survival does not equal ideological victory.
- Assassination sometimes does destroy organisations or political programmes; Gandhi cannot be universalised from one case without comparison.

### Biggest Vulnerability

The book may become too theoretical or universal if it overstates what Gandhi's case proves about political assassination generally. Comparative cases would be necessary if the argument claims more than Gandhi alone can establish.

### What Additional Research Is Needed

- Precise reconstruction of Godse's strategic objective from his primary statements.
- Comparison with at least a small number of other political assassinations, chosen only if necessary to test the claim.
- Scholarship on martyrdom, political memory and assassination.
- Evidence showing how the assassination changed rather than merely followed Gandhi's symbolic status.

### Potential Book Structure

**Part I:** What the assassin believed killing Gandhi would achieve.

**Part II:** The assassination and destruction of the living political actor.

**Part III:** The creation of the posthumous Gandhi.

**Part IV:** Why the assassin could not control that afterlife.

**Part V:** The limits of assassination as a political technology.

## 10. Thesis Option E

### Thesis / Central Proposition

**Rebuilt Original:** Gandhi was killed in a political and ideological conflict, but killing him did not erase the meanings attached to him. Instead, his death transformed those meanings into a contested legacy that later generations could preserve, attack, appropriate, reject, neutralise or reinterpret. The historical struggle therefore did continue — but not as one uninterrupted campaign by one organisation or ideology. It continued as a recurring contest over what Gandhi should mean in India and beyond.

### Core Historical Claim

The strongest part of the original intuition survives if the claim changes from **continuous destruction** to **continuing contestation**. Gandhi was physically eliminated as an active political actor; his symbolic and moral presence survived; and later political actors repeatedly fought over the content of that survival.

### Main Research Question

**After Gandhi was killed, what exactly were later political actors fighting over when they fought about Gandhi?**

### What Makes It Different

This is the closest option to the author's original emotional architecture. It retains assassination, survival and continuing conflict, but removes the unsupported claim that “the same forces” remain continuously at war with Gandhi.

### Evidence Supporting It

- Strong ideological and political motive evidence in Godse's own primary statements.
- Strong evidence of a wider conspiracy.
- Strong evidence of Gandhi's enduring symbolic survival.
- Strong evidence of continuing post-1948 contest over Gandhi's meaning.
- Evidence of specific counter-memory, including fringe Godse glorification.
- Evidence of selective appropriation and possible neutralisation in narrower cases.

### Evidence That Complicates It

- The post-1948 field includes defenders, critics, appropriators, neutralisers and rejectors whose motivations are not identical.
- State commemoration strongly contradicts a generalized erasure narrative.
- Modern actors cannot be treated as automatic descendants of the 1948 conspirators or ideological environment.
- Gandhi's political programme itself changed and fragmented in the afterlife.

### Biggest Vulnerability

The option can still drift back toward the original unsupported continuity claim if the language “the struggle continued” is treated as one struggle with one actor. The book would need disciplined periodisation and actor-specific case studies.

### What Additional Research Is Needed

- Identify a small number of modern cases where continuity is genuinely arguable.
- Separate those cases from independent criticism and appropriation.
- Establish whether any specific institutions or texts demonstrate inheritance from the 1948 ideological environment.
- Develop criteria for “contestation,” “neutralisation” and “counter-memory” before using them analytically.

### Potential Book Structure

**Part I:** Gandhi as living political actor and the conflict surrounding him.

**Part II:** The assassination — what was being attacked and what Godse believed he was achieving.

**Part III:** The first transformation — Gandhi becomes a national and moral symbol.

**Part IV:** The long contest — criticism, appropriation, rejection, neutralisation and counter-memory.

**Part V:** The unresolved question of inheritance — whether Gandhi can be possessed, erased, or permanently fixed by any one political tradition.

## 12. Strongest Surviving Core Idea

The original idea should not be discarded. It should be **translated from a claim about one continuous enemy into a claim about the afterlife of a political meaning**.

The strongest surviving core is:

> **Gandhi was killed in an ideological and political conflict, but his death did not end the political struggle over what Gandhi meant.**

That formulation preserves the essential intuition while avoiding unsupported assumptions about organisational continuity.

A second, equally important insight is:

> **The thing that survived Gandhi's assassination was not one single “Gandhi.” Different parts of Gandhi survived in different forms.**

This is why “contested inheritance” is stronger than “legacy survived” in the abstract.

The research suggests that at least six distinct Gandhis can be analytically separated:

1. **Gandhi the national symbol.**
2. **Gandhi the moral authority.**
3. **Gandhi the anti-colonial political thinker.**
4. **Gandhi the advocate of nonviolence and communal reconciliation.**
5. **Gandhi the critic of modern industrial and centralised political life.**
6. **Gandhi the controversial historical figure whose caste, race, political and personal record can be seriously criticised.**

The post-1948 struggle is partly a struggle over which of these versions gets preserved, rejected or made politically useful.

That is a more historically precise way of preserving the author's original intuition that something was being fought over beyond Gandhi's physical body.

## 13. Recommended Direction for Further Testing

The Product Manager should **not yet declare one thesis final**. The strongest next step is to test the top three in direct adversarial comparison:

### Test Option C — Contested Inheritance

Question:

**Can the book identify a finite number of historically significant inheritance struggles in which actors explicitly claimed, rejected, transformed or neutralised particular parts of Gandhi?**

Falsification condition:

If the supposedly competing inheritances cannot be demonstrated through actors' own statements, practices or documented institutional decisions, the concept becomes authorial interpretation rather than historical argument.

### Test Option A — Assassination → Afterlife

Question:

**Can the assassination be shown to have materially transformed Gandhi's political meaning rather than merely followed a pre-existing culture of Gandhi veneration?**

Falsification condition:

If posthumous Gandhi is essentially identical to pre-assassination Gandhi in political meaning, the causal importance of the assassination becomes weaker.

### Test Option E — Rebuilt Original

Question:

**Can the book identify specific later conflicts where the post-1948 struggle over Gandhi can be shown to descend from, inherit, or consciously respond to the political conflict surrounding the assassination?**

Falsification condition:

If modern examples repeatedly turn out to be independent political disputes with no meaningful historical transmission, the continuity element should be removed even from the rebuilt version.

### Recommended conceptual experiment

For each candidate, build a matrix with:

**actor → period → claim about Gandhi → what the actor adopts → what the actor rejects → evidence → relation to 1948 conflict → type of relationship (inheritance / appropriation / criticism / rejection / neutralisation / counter-memory / analogy).**

Whichever concept survives that matrix with the least special pleading should become the basis of the final Checkpoint 1 thesis.

## 14. Research Needed Before Final Concept Acceptance

Only research that could materially alter the concept should be prioritised.

### 14.1 Post-1948 inheritance case studies

Identify approximately 5–8 major cases across different decades and political traditions. For each case, determine whether the actor explicitly invokes Gandhi, selectively adopts him, rejects him, or attempts to redefine him.

This is the most important missing evidence for choosing between Options A, C and E.

### 14.2 Continuity test for modern anti-Gandhi activity

Select the strongest modern examples that appear to support the original thesis and test for:

- organisational continuity;
- textual transmission;
- personnel continuity;
- institutional inheritance;
- repeated explicit objectives;
- direct references to the 1948 conflict or Godse.

The outcome may either strengthen a narrower continuity argument or eliminate it.

### 14.3 Godse's strategic objective

The book should determine from the complete primary record whether Godse primarily sought:

- immediate policy change;
- elimination of Gandhi's moral authority;
- alteration of Indian state policy;
- prevention of further political concessions;
- or a broader symbolic intervention.

This matters especially for Option D.

### 14.4 Savarkar and organisation-level evidence

The thesis does not need to settle every Savarkar or RSS question, but any version that places organisational ideology at the centre must have a precise evidentiary matrix.

Remaining questions include:

- Godse's exact RSS status in January 1948;
- organisation-level evidence concerning RSS knowledge or authorization;
- Hindu Mahasabha institutional responsibility;
- the exact weight of the Kapur Commission;
- and the full documentary context surrounding Savarkar.

### 14.5 Gandhi's final programme

Option B especially requires a more systematic comparison between Gandhi's January 1948 political vision and the Gandhi represented in later public memory.

The 29 January Lok Sevak Sangh proposal should be treated as a particularly important primary anchor.
