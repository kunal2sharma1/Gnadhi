## 1. Research Question

What were Gandhi's actual political priorities, positions, and interventions during his final months, roughly August 1947 through 30 January 1948, and what do primary/contemporaneous sources show about what he was trying to achieve immediately before his assassination?

## 2. Scope

Focus on Calcutta and Delhi communal peace efforts, Partition, Hindu-Muslim relations, India-Pakistan relations, the Rs. 55 crore dispute, the September 1947 and January 1948 fasts, nonviolence, retaliation, minority rights, and final political priorities.

## 3. Key Findings

- Gandhi spent the final period of his life primarily on communal peacekeeping in Calcutta and Delhi, not on holding formal state office.
- He undertook two major final fasts: Calcutta in September 1947 and Delhi in January 1948. Both were explicitly connected to communal peace.
- The January 1948 fast is often remembered as a fast for payment to Pakistan, but the documentary record is more layered. Protection and restoration of rights for Delhi's Muslims, mosques, shrines, and safe movement were central announced conditions; the Rs. 55 crore payment was a closely related contemporaneous government decision that Gandhi supported and that became politically fused with the fast.
- Gandhi repeatedly demanded restraint and protection for minorities, while also acknowledging Hindu and Sikh suffering in Pakistan and placing obligations on Muslims remaining in India.
- Gandhi remained opposed to Partition in principle, describing it as the vivisection of India, but did not obstruct Congress's final decision to accept it when he believed public opinion had changed.
- Gandhi's final statements include genuine complications to any simplified portrait of absolute pacifism. On Kashmir, he described war as a curse but supported Indian military action to protect civilians.
- Gandhi's final political draft proposed transforming Congress into a decentralised social-service organisation (Lok Sevak Sangh), suggesting a final concern with constructive moral work rather than retaining electoral power.

## 4. Chronology of the Final Period

### August–September 1947: Calcutta

Gandhi reached Calcutta in August 1947 amid communal danger and did not treat Independence Day as a conventional celebration. Renewed violence led to his September fast. The fast ended after community leaders pledged to stop violence. This episode is important because it demonstrates the kind of political method Gandhi used in his final period: moral authority, physical self-suffering, direct negotiation, public prayer, and local intervention.

### September 1947 onward: Delhi

Gandhi reached Delhi in September and found a refugee and communal crisis. He visited camps and neighbourhoods, including mosques being used by refugees, and held interfaith prayer meetings that included Quranic readings. These were controversial among some attendees.

He directly addressed RSS audiences in September 1947, praising discipline while criticising exclusionary interpretations of Indian nationhood. In a November 1947 prayer meeting he explicitly criticised Hindu Mahasabha and RSS rhetoric that sought to drive Muslims out of the Indian Union.

### January 1948 fast

On 13 January Gandhi began his final fast unto death. His stated framing emphasised communal peace and shared wrongdoing. Contemporary government records also show his support for releasing the Rs. 55 crore payment to Pakistan, even while India and Pakistan were in conflict over Kashmir.

The Government of India announced payment during the fast. Gandhi broke the fast on 18 January after community representatives, including Hindu and Sikh leaders and representatives associated with RSS/Hindu Mahasabha, pledged to protect Delhi's Muslims and restore normal civic and religious life.

### 20 January 1948

A bomb attempt occurred during Gandhi's prayer meeting. Gandhi survived and continued his activity. The event is crucial to the later assassination record and demonstrates that hostility toward Gandhi had become operationally violent before 30 January.

### Final days

Gandhi worked on the final Congress/Lok Sevak Sangh draft, attempted to reconcile political tensions involving Nehru and Patel, and still hoped to visit Pakistan. His political life remained focused on reconciliation, communal peace, and moral reconstruction.

## 5. Gandhi on Communal Violence

Gandhi's final interventions were practical and local rather than merely rhetorical. He moved among affected communities, visited refugee camps and Muslim areas, and used prayer meetings and fasting as instruments of moral pressure.

His stated objective was not simply to protect Muslims. He repeatedly demanded restraint from Hindus and Sikhs while also requiring Muslims remaining in India to demonstrate loyalty to the Indian Union. He rejected retaliatory logic on all sides.

The important nuance is that his visible demands often focused on Muslims in Delhi because Muslims were a vulnerable minority facing displacement and violence. This asymmetry of intervention should not be misread automatically as hostility to Hindus; the record also contains explicit acknowledgement of Hindu and Sikh suffering in Pakistan.

## 6. Gandhi on Hindu-Muslim Relations

Gandhi defended the position that Muslims remaining in India should be full and equal citizens, not tolerated residents. His multi-faith prayer meetings deliberately used Hindu, Muslim, and Sikh religious texts. The Quran readings themselves became politically controversial among some attendees.

This position is central to understanding the hostility he faced from Hindu-nationalist circles. At the same time, his approach was not simply a one-sided defence of Muslims: his speeches demanded reciprocal duties and explicitly condemned Muslim communal violence as well.

## 7. Gandhi on Partition

Gandhi remained opposed to Partition in principle. He had long used the language of "vivisection" and argued that India should not be divided on communal lines.

However, when Congress leadership accepted Partition, Gandhi did not launch a coercive political struggle against the decision. He argued that once public opinion had changed, forcing the country back toward his preferred position would itself violate his principles.

This distinction matters: opposition to Partition continued as a moral position, while political obstruction diminished.

## 8. Gandhi on India-Pakistan and Rs. 55 Crore

The withheld Rs. 55 crore represented Pakistan's agreed share of pre-Partition financial assets. During the Kashmir conflict, the Government of India decided to withhold the payment. Gandhi supported releasing it despite the ongoing conflict, arguing that India should honour an agreed obligation rather than use payment as retaliatory leverage.

This issue became one of the central grievances cited by Godse. However, the research shows that the January fast cannot safely be described as simply "the fast for Pakistan's money." The formal and publicly stated conditions concerned communal peace in Delhi, protection of Muslims, restoration of mosques and shrines, safe movement, and return rights. The financial decision happened in the same period and Gandhi explicitly supported it, but the two questions should not be collapsed.

This distinction is a major future verification priority using the complete January 1948 prayer speeches and CWMG Vol. 90.

## 9. Gandhi on Nonviolence, Retaliation, and State Force

Gandhi repeatedly condemned revenge and retaliation during the final period. He argued that communal violence degraded the perpetrators regardless of religion.

But Gandhi's nonviolence was not equivalent to absolute pacifism toward every use of state force. His support for Indian military action in Kashmir, framed as protection of innocent civilians, complicates simple definitions of Gandhian nonviolence.

His June 1947 reflection that Indian nonviolence might have been "the ahimsa of the impotent" also shows self-questioning about the distinction between principled nonviolence and strategic nonviolence adopted under conditions of limited alternatives.

## 10. Gandhi's Political Methods and Authority

Fasting functioned as a form of moral pressure. Gandhi described it as an act of conscience rather than a political manoeuvre, but critics argued that his extraordinary personal authority gave the fast an unavoidable political coercive effect.

This is a serious criticism that should be addressed rather than dismissed. The fact that Gandhi accepted personal physical risk does not by itself resolve the question of whether others were placed under moral pressure to concede.

## 11. Final Political Priorities

The final Lok Sevak Sangh draft proposed dissolving Congress in its existing political form and transforming it into a decentralised social-service organisation. The exact date of the draft is disputed in the research (27 versus 29 January 1948) and should be checked directly against CWMG Vol. 90.

Gandhi's final political priorities therefore appear to have centred on communal reconciliation, minority protection, moral reconstruction, and social service rather than acquisition or retention of formal political power.

## 12. What the Evidence Safely Supports

- Gandhi repeatedly and publicly defended minority rights and communal peace during his final months.
- His interventions placed obligations on Hindus, Sikhs, and Muslims rather than exclusively on one community.
- He opposed Partition in principle but accepted Congress's political decision once he believed public opinion had shifted.
- He supported paying Pakistan the withheld Rs. 55 crore.
- The January fast should not be reduced to the Rs. 55 crore payment alone.
- He used fasting and moral authority as political methods even while describing them as acts of conscience.
- His final political programme moved toward decentralised social service rather than conventional party politics.
- His relationship to nonviolence was complicated and not identical to blanket rejection of all state force.

## 13. What the Evidence Does NOT Safely Support

- That Gandhi was anti-Hindu or indifferent to Hindu/Sikh suffering.
- That Gandhi single-handedly caused Partition.
- That the January 1948 fast was simply or solely about Pakistan's money.
- That Gandhi's final political position can be reduced to one slogan such as "appeasement."
- That his nonviolence was a simple, internally consistent policy of absolute pacifism.

## 14. Unresolved Questions and Research Gaps

- Exact weight Gandhi himself placed on Rs. 55 crore as part of the January fast.
- Complete January 1948 prayer speeches and *Harijan* material in CWMG Vol. 90.
- Direct consultation of Pyarelal's *The Last Phase* and major biographies.
- Exact date and status of the Lok Sevak Sangh draft.
- Detailed plans for Gandhi's proposed visit to Pakistan.
- How representative ordinary refugee anger was compared with organised RSS/Hindu Mahasabha opposition.
- Full correspondence with Jinnah and other political leaders in the final period.
